Multi-Season Projects — how the work actually moved

Shakti · forestbuilder · 27 Linear tickets · 24 PRs · 129 reviews · 223 Pi sessions · 2026-09-28 → 2026-10-22

The plan was written in one sitting. The code was built in four days. Review was two sweeps, two days apart. Twenty of the twenty-three merges happened in a hundred and eight minutes. Authoring was never the constraint, and the sessions almost never name the ticket they are working on. That gap — between a graph of tickets and a stream of PR-anchored conversations — is the space a room has to occupy.

1 · The shape

Two bursts and two trains. Twenty-two PRs opened between 10-13 and 10-16; the first review landed about two days later; the merges ran as one train of nine at 16:08 and one of eleven at 17:53 on 10-22. PR #1712 (the seed-id fix) was closed unmerged and redone as #1740, which is why one branch appears twice. Nothing else was reverted.

2 · The unit is the thread

Ed reviewed in blockers, not summaries: “Three merge blockers.”, “One merge blocker, inline.”, “Two merge blockers, inline.” Each blocker is anchored at a path and a line, and each got a reply that begins with Fixed: and names the symbol that changed. Fifty-nine root threads, fifty-nine replies. The PR is a bag of threads; the ticket is a chapter heading.

This is why a room that routes PRs will feel wrong. The thing you pick up is a line, with its history and its commit; the thing the agent changes is a symbol; the thing Ed checks next is the same line again. A ticket-level notification loses the anchor, and the anchor is the only reason the follow-up session can start without re-reading the PR.

3 · The plan is a living graph

DEV-1496 is a plan ticket whose body holds the dependency tree, the decisions, and an explicit “not doing” list. Fourteen of the twenty-five bodies read carry a Drift check, 2026-10-13 — a paragraph naming a commit and the file:line places the plan has moved under. That is a human running a conformance check against the tree, by hand, in prose.

Eleven of the twenty-seven tickets were created after the first PR. Two came straight out of review threads (1522, 1523), the rest out of the first pass of work. A room that treats the plan as fixed will either refuse the new work or file it somewhere the stack cannot see; a room that treats the plan as a graph can attach a new node to the layer it belongs to and re-derive what is reviewable.

4 · The sessions

Across seventy-six days: 223 sessions, 2,947 user turns, 44,498 tool calls, $89. In the three weeks of this project: 40 sessions, 528 user turns, 9,012 programs, 47 active hours inside 339 hours of open session. Almost all of the wall time is a session left open while a human is elsewhere.

Two facts change the design. First, 44,433 of 44,498 tool calls are fabric_exec — the agent writes a program that calls many tools, so a per-tool hook sees almost nothing of what happened; the fact stream has to come from the program's effects or from the fabric runtime. Second, five of forty sessions name a DEV id, and the sessions begin with a PR URL, a review permalink (#discussion_r…), or a pasted Slack thread. The human does not enter through the ticket. The room has to derive the chit from the anchor and never ask.

5 · “Ready to deploy” is not “done”

Every merged leaf sits in Ready to deploy; not one ticket has a completion date. Twenty-one of twenty-seven are in that state. The four parents — the plan, a phase, the site-status chapter, the list chapter — are still In Progress even though their children merged, and one phase parent is still Backlog. The GitHub integration moved the ticket when the PR merged; the deployment gate is a later, separate act. A room that equates merge with done will report a project complete while it is waiting on a deploy — and the whole point of the state is that a human still owns it.

6 · What the room takes from this

Evidence Primitive
5 of 40 sessions name a ticket; they open from a PR or a review permalink Address the thread. A chit is born from (PR, path, line, commit); the ticket is a parent, never the entry point.
59 blockers, 59 replies; 3–4 arrive in one sweep; median reply 43 h Route a blocker set. One turn carries the whole sweep; the room shows unanswered threads, not unread PRs.
20 of 23 merged in 108 minutes, 7 days after opening Hold the stack. “Reviewable” is a property of a layer — the one below merged — not of a PR.
10 tickets discovered after the first PR Let the graph grow. A session can mint a chit and attach it to a layer; the plan is re-derived, not edited by hand.
14 of 25 bodies carry a manual drift check with file:line Automate the drift. Ticket anchors against the tree is a jig engine: re-check on every merge, not on a hunch.
44,433 of 44,498 calls are fabric_exec Tap the program, not the tool loop. Facts come from the effects (paths, commands, commits) or from fabric itself.
21 tickets in Ready to deploy, 0 Done Merge ≠ done. The room’s terminal state is deployed, and a human closes it.
47 active hours inside 339 open hours Presence is a heartbeat. Session open is not availability; only a lease says who may act.

The one-sentence model: a human plans a graph in one sitting, an agent turns each node into a stacked PR, a reviewer turns each PR into a handful of line-anchored blockers, and a merge train closes the layer — so the room should track threads and layers, and let tickets, PRs and sessions be projections of them.
